Today, I stumbled upon a video that made a profound comparison: **AI is like a microwave.** 

As an Enterprise Architect, I hear a lot of analogies for Artificial Intelligence. Most of them frame AI as a conscious entity, an all-knowing oracle, or an unstoppable tsunami that will wash away how we work. But the microwave? That instantly made sense. 

Think about the microwave for a moment. It is an incredible piece of technology. If you tried explaining it to someone from 100 years ago, a box that heats food from the inside out using invisible waves in under a minute, you wouldn’t even know where to start. Today, it’s in almost every home. But here is the critical part: we still don’t cook every meal in it. 

Over time, we learned what a microwave is actually good at, and what it completely ruins. 

That’s exactly where we are with Artificial Intelligence today.

## The Kitchen Wiring Problem

From an architectural perspective, there is another layer to this analogy. Even the most advanced, feature-rich microwave on the market is completely useless if your kitchen’s wiring is broken or the local power grid is unreliable. 

Right now, a lot of organizations are attempting to force-fit AI into everything. Every workflow. Every team. Every product. We are treating it like a plug-and-play appliance, ignoring the state of the house we are plugging it into. You simply cannot drop a high-performance AI capability into an enterprise if your underlying data governance, security posture, and integration architecture are a mess. 

If your data is siloed and your APIs are brittle, the AI won't act as a magic fix; it will just process your operational dysfunction faster.

## The Illusion of Instant Magic

There is an overwhelming market pressure driving this frantic adoption. The prevailing assumption across the tech landscape is incredibly dangerous in its simplicity: *If we just drop AI in, magic will happen.*

> "The expectation is set: integrate AI, and the product will instantly be superior, productivity will skyrocket, investors will be thrilled, stock prices will surge, and record profits will follow."

We are treating AI like a financial wand rather than an operational tool. Market and investor sentiment are demanding AI features on every roadmap, often prioritizing the announcement of the feature over its actual utility. We are building AI into products not because the user problem demands it, but because the next funding round or quarterly earnings call requires it. 

## The Boardroom vs. Reality

Despite the hype, the reality on the ground is much more grounded: **We are still squarely in the experimentation phase.**

As architects and engineers, we’ve found a few things AI is genuinely, undeniably useful for. Code generation, log analysis, and initial content drafting are proving real value. But we’ve also found a massively long list of places where the vendor demo looks absolutely brilliant, yet the actual business case stays remarkably fuzzy. 

That is completely normal. That is exactly how new tools settle into a complex enterprise ecosystem. 

Companies are feeling this friction. What’s being shared in boardrooms right now is often an estimate, a projection, or a hopeful narrative, not a proven, scalable result. Massive budgets are going in, but the Return on Investment (ROI) is still largely being guessed. 

## The Productivity Gap

This brings us to a fundamental truth that every Enterprise Architect eventually learns: optimizing a silo doesn't fix a system. 

It is entirely possible for individual productivity to go up while company earnings barely move. We see it all the time. An employee might use generative AI to write a report in 10 minutes instead of two hours. But if that report still has to sit in an approval queue for three weeks because of archaic compliance workflows, the business hasn't actually moved any faster.

A faster tool at one isolated desk doesn't fix a broken end-to-end enterprise process. 

## The Pragmatic Path Forward

None of this is a reason to ignore AI. In fact, it is the exact opposite. It’s a reason to stop treating every single AI use case as a finished, market-ready conclusion.

The organizations that will ultimately get this right won’t be the ones making the loudest, most aggressive predictions. They won't be the ones attempting massive, high-risk "rip-and-replace" transformations just to appease market hype and artificially inflate their stock prices. 

They will be the companies taking a pragmatic, architectural approach:
1. **Running small, validated experiments** to find real value.
2. **Fixing the foundation** to ensure their data architecture actually supports the load.
3. **Being brutally honest** about what works, and more importantly, what doesn’t.

We’ll figure out what AI is actually for, the exact same way we figured out the microwave. We will find its perfect use cases, and we will stop trying to use it to bake a cake. 

We’re just not done experimenting yet.
